Exercises: Branch and Bound

These exercises review the two lessons on branch and bound. They are different from the seminar activities and from the lab problems, which are left for those sessions. Try each one before reading its solution: the solutions are grouped at the end of the page.

Give the order in which the nodes are expanded with (a) depth-first search, (b) breadth-first search and (c) best-first search. (d) In best-first search, suppose that G turns out to be a solution with cost 11, and that the estimates are valid lower bounds. Which nodes are never expanded?

8.3. Valid bounds for assignment. In the task assignment problem (minimize the total cost), three lower bounds for a partial assignment are proposed. For each, say whether it is valid (never overestimates) and why:

  • (a) cost so far plus the minimum of each free column (task) over the free agents;
  • (b) cost so far plus the minimum of each free row (agent) over the free tasks;
  • (c) cost so far plus the maximum of (a) and (b).

8.4. Admissible heuristics. For the 8-puzzle, say whether each heuristic is admissible (never exceeds the true number of moves): (a) the number of misplaced tiles; (b) twice the number of misplaced tiles; (c) the Manhattan distance, counting the empty cell as a tile. Give a counterexample for those that are not.

Solutions​

8.1.

  • (a) Depth-first: A, B, D, E, C, F, H, G.
  • (b) Breadth-first: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H.
  • (c) Best-first: A (8), C (9), F (10), G (11), B (12), E (12), H (13), D (14). After expanding F, its child H (13) enters the queue but waits behind G and B. Between B and E, both with 12, B goes first because E does not exist until B is expanded.
  • (d) Once G gives a solution of cost 11, every live node with estimate 11 or more can be discarded: B, D, E and H are never expanded. Only A, C, F and G are.

8.3.

  • (a) Valid: each free task will be done by some free agent, at least at the cheapest cost of its column. This is the bound of the lesson.
  • (b) Valid, by the same argument applied to agents: each free agent will do some free task, at least at the cheapest cost of its row.
  • (c) Valid: if two values are both lower bounds, their maximum is also a lower bound, and it is tighter (better) than either. It costs twice as much to compute.

↑ Back to exercise 8.3

8.4.

  • (a) Admissible: each misplaced tile needs at least one move.
  • (b) Not admissible: the board 1 2 3 / 4 5 6 / 7 . 8 has one misplaced tile and is solved in one move, but the heuristic gives 2.
  • (c) Not admissible: on the same board, the tiles contribute 1 (the 8) and the empty cell contributes 1 more (it is one column away from its place), so the heuristic gives 2 for a board solved in 1 move. That is why the empty cell must not be counted.

↑ Back to exercise 8.4

8.5. For the 3 × 3 board, the goal is reachable if and only if the number of inversions among the 8 tiles (ignoring the empty cell) is even.

  • (a) The tiles in order are 1 2 3 4 5 6 8 7: one inversion (8, 7). Odd: not reachable. This is the famous puzzle with which Sam Loyd, in its 15-puzzle version, offered a prize that nobody could claim.
  • (b) 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8: no inversions. Reachable (in one move).
  • (c) 8 1 2 4 3 7 6 5: the 8 forms 7 inversions, (4, 3) one more, and among 7 6 5 there are 3. In total 11, odd: not reachable.

↑ Back to exercise 8.5

8.6.

  • (a) Backtracking: every solution must be listed, so the whole tree is explored anyway, and backtracking uses much less memory.
  • (b) Branch and bound (A*) with priority g+hg + hg+h and the Manhattan distance: it finds the shortest solution, while depth-first search could get lost in very long paths.
  • (c) Backtracking: it only stores the current path. Constraint pruning and choosing the cell with fewest candidates first make it fast for Sudoku.
  • (d) Branch and bound: with a good bound and a best-first order, it can discard most of the 20!≈2.4×101820! \approx 2.4 \times 10^{18}20!≈2.4×1018 assignments.
