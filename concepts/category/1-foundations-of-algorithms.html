1. Foundations of Algorithms

The foundations of algorithmics: what an algorithm is and how to specify it, the data structures that support efficient processing, and the tools to measure and analyze the cost of algorithms (empirical measurement, counting operations, asymptotic notation, growth rates and loop analysis), applied to classic case studies such as the maximum pairwise product and searching.

Algorithmic Thinking

In computer science, programming syntax is merely a vehicle for execution. The true engineering core lies in Algorithmics: the systematic study, design and analysis of correct and efficient computational strategies.

Case Study - Pairwise Product

To truly understand why algorithmics matters, it helps to experience failure first. In this case study we take a deceptively simple problem and solve it again and again. Each version fixes a problem of the previous one, and each fix teaches a different lesson: about the limits of data types, the cost of naive strategies, the impact of data representation, and the danger of "optimizations" that are not actually correct.

Data Structures

In the previous lessons we saw that the efficiency of a solution depends on the strategy we choose. It also depends, just as much, on how the data is organized in memory. The maximum pairwise product already gave us a hint: the same algorithm used several times more memory with a List than with an int[].

Measuring Efficiency

In the case study we compared four strategies for the maximum pairwise product and saw differences ranging from seconds to fractions of a millisecond. But how exactly should we compare algorithms? Is it enough to run them and look at the clock? What should we measure, and how can we trust the numbers?

Complexity Analysis

In the previous lesson we saw that measurements depend on the machine, the compiler and the implementation, and that comparing algorithms only by running them has serious limitations. We now develop the alternative: studying the algorithm on paper, by counting how many elementary operations it performs as a function of the size of its input.

Asymptotic Notation

In the previous lesson we obtained exact expressions such as $T(n) = 3n + 4$ and saw that the constants depend on arbitrary counting decisions and, in reality, on the machine. What does not depend on them is the rate of growth of the function: how fast $T(n)$ increases when $n$ becomes large.

Growth Rates and Predictions

Asymptotic notation gives us a precise language. This lesson turns it into practical intuition: what the most common complexity classes mean in real time, how to predict the time an algorithm will need for a larger input, how to estimate the largest input it can handle in a given time, and why no improvement in hardware can change the complexity of an algorithm.

Complexity of Loops

Most iterative algorithms are, at their core, combinations of loops. Once you can determine quickly and reliably how many times a loop runs, you can analyze almost any iterative program. This lesson collects the most common patterns, explains how to derive each result and points out the traps that appear most often in exams and in real code.

Searching Case Studies

Searching is one of the most frequent operations in computing: looking up a user in a database, a word in a dictionary, a file in a directory, a value in a cache. In this final lesson of the chapter we apply everything we have learnt to three classic search algorithms. For each one we count instructions exactly, analyze the best, worst and average cases, express the result with asymptotic notation and connect it with real measurements.
