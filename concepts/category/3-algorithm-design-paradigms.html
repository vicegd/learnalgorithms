3. Algorithm Design Paradigms

The main algorithm design paradigms: brute force, divide and conquer (scheme, recurrence analysis and many classic problems), greedy algorithms and dynamic programming.

📄️Brute Force and Exhaustive Search

Brute force is the most direct way to design an algorithm: solve the problem by doing exactly what its statement says, with no attempt to exploit its structure. To find an element, look at every position. To find the best combination, generate every combination and keep the best one. To check whether a text contains a word, try the word at every position of the text.

📄️Divide and Conquer

Divide and conquer is one of the oldest and most powerful strategies for designing algorithms. Instead of attacking a problem as a whole, it breaks it into smaller problems of the same kind, solves them (usually by applying the same strategy again), and combines their solutions. We have already met two examples: binary search, which discards half of the array at each step, and Quicksort, which partitions the array and sorts each part.

📄️Analyzing Divide and Conquer

The loops of iterative algorithms can be analyzed by counting iterations, as we did in the first chapter. Recursive algorithms need a different tool: the execution time is expressed in terms of itself applied to smaller sizes, giving a recurrence equation. Fortunately, most divide and conquer algorithms follow one of two very regular patterns, and for both of them the solution is known in general. This lesson presents those two patterns, explains where their formulas come from, and shows how to apply them.

📄️Divide and Conquer: Numbers and Searches

This lesson applies the paradigm and its analysis to a first set of classic problems: the factorial, sequential and binary search, the Fibonacci numbers and the greatest common divisor. Some of them show the strength of divide and conquer; others, especially Fibonacci, show what happens when it is applied badly. All of them are implemented, with tests, in the course repository.

📄️Divide and Conquer: Classic Problems

This lesson presents some of the most famous divide and conquer algorithms. Two of them, Mergesort and Quicksort, we already know from the sorting chapter; now we can analyze them with the formulas of this chapter. The others (the Towers of Hanoi, the multiplication of large integers, the tennis tournament and the Tromino puzzle) show how the paradigm solves problems that would be hard to approach in any other way.

📄️Divide and Conquer: Majority, Mode and Median

The last group of problems of this chapter comes from statistics this lesson develops the median completely, and leaves the divide and conquer versions of the majority element and the mode as challenges, with hints.

📄️Greedy Algorithms

Many problems ask not just for a solution, but for the best one they build the solution step by step and, at each step, they take the choice that looks best right now, without ever reconsidering it.

📄️Greedy Algorithms: Classic Problems

This lesson applies the greedy strategy to a series of classic problems. For some of them, a good heuristic gives the optimal solution, and we will see why. For others, it gives only a good solution, and a counterexample shows it. Learning to tell both situations apart is the main goal. The implementations are in the course repository.

📄️Exercises: Greedy Algorithms

📄️Dynamic Programming

Divide and conquer breaks a problem into subproblems and combines their solutions. It works beautifully when the subproblems are independent. But we saw in the divide and conquer chapter that the naive recursive Fibonacci solves the same subproblems again and again, and becomes exponential. Dynamic programming keeps the idea of solving a problem through its subproblems, but solves each subproblem only once, storing its solution in a table for later use.

📄️Dynamic Programming: Classic Problems

This lesson applies the design steps of dynamic programming to several classic problems the change and the cheapest travel on a river. The implementations of the course examples are in the course repository.
