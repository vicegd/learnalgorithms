# Asymptotic Notation: Describing How Cost Grows

In the previous lesson we obtained exact expressions such as $T(n) = 3n + 4$ and saw that the constants depend on arbitrary counting decisions and, in reality, on the machine. What does *not* depend on them is the **rate of growth** of the function: how fast $T(n)$ increases when $n$ becomes large.

**Asymptotic notation** is the mathematical language for describing that rate of growth. It refers to the speed of growth of the values of a function $f(n)$ as $n$ tends to infinity, ignoring constant factors and lower-order terms. It is the notation used in every textbook, paper and technical interview to talk about the efficiency of algorithms.

:::note What you will learn in this lesson
- The formal definition of $O$ (big-O) and how to prove that a function belongs to $O(f(n))$.
- The related notations $\Omega$ (big-Omega) and $\Theta$ (big-Theta), and what each one really says.
- The algebraic properties that let us simplify expressions without going back to the definition.
- The logarithm rules you need for analyzing algorithms, and why the base of a logarithm does not matter.
- How to determine the order of complex expressions quickly and reliably.
:::

## Big-O: An Upper Bound on Growth

**Definition.** A function $T(n)$ is $O(f(n))$ if there exist two positive constants $c$ and $n_0$ such that:

$$
T(n) \le c \cdot f(n) \qquad \text{for all } n \ge n_0
$$

In words: from some point $n_0$ onwards, $T(n)$ never exceeds a constant multiple of $f(n)$. If $T(n)$ is $O(f(n))$, we say that $T(n)$ has, **at most**, a growth rate of $f(n)$.

</Figure>

Two details of the definition are important:

- **The constant $c$** absorbs the constant factors: $3n$, $100n$ and $n/2$ are all $O(n)$. This is exactly the freedom we need, since constants depend on the machine and on the counting conventions.
- **The threshold $n_0$** means that only the behavior for **large** values of $n$ matters. What happens for small inputs is irrelevant to the definition.

### Proving a bound: an example

Let us prove that $T(n) = 5n^4 + 4n^3$ is $O(n^4)$. We need constants $c$ and $n_0$ such that $5n^4 + 4n^3 \le c \cdot n^4$ for every $n \ge n_0$.

The idea is to bound the lower-order term by the dominant one. For $n \ge 4$ we have $4n^3 \le n \cdot n^3 = n^4$, therefore:

$$
5n^4 + 4n^3 \le 5n^4 + n^4 = 6n^4 \qquad \text{for all } n \ge 4
$$

So the definition holds with $c = 6$ and $n_0 = 4$ (or any larger $n_0$, such as $n_0 = 5$). The values of $c$ and $n_0$ are not unique: with $c = 9$, for example, the inequality holds from $n_0 = 1$, because $4n^3 \le 4n^4$ for $n \ge 1$. We only need to show that **some** pair exists.

:::tip A quicker test with limits
When the limit exists, we can compare growth rates with:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{T(n)}{f(n)} = L
$$

- If $L$ is a **finite** number (including 0), then $T(n)$ is $O(f(n))$.
- If $L = 0$, $T(n)$ grows **strictly more slowly** than $f(n)$.
- If $L$ is a finite **positive** number, both functions grow at the **same rate**.
- If $L = \infty$, $T(n)$ grows faster, and it is **not** $O(f(n))$.

For example, $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{5n^4 + 4n^3}{n^4} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(5 + \frac{4}{n}\right) = 5$, so both functions grow at the same rate.
:::

### Big-O is a bound, not an exact description

Since $O$ is an upper bound, a function belongs to many classes: $3n + 4$ is $O(n)$, but also $O(n^2)$ and $O(2^n)$, because all those functions grow at least as fast as $n$. These statements are true but not very informative. When we analyze an algorithm, we always give the **tightest** bound we can prove: saying that the sum of a vector is $O(n^2)$ is correct, but misleading.

:::caution Big-O does not mean "worst case"
A common misconception is that $O$ describes the worst case. It does not: $O$, $\Omega$ and $\Theta$ describe **functions**, and *any* of the functions we studied in the previous lesson can be described with them. The worst-case time of linear search is $O(n)$, its best-case time is $O(1)$, its average-case time is $O(n)$. When someone says that "an algorithm is $O(n^2)$" without further detail, they usually mean that its **worst-case** time is $O(n^2)$, but the two ideas are independent.
:::

## Big-Omega and Big-Theta

Big-O gives an upper bound. Two related notations complete the picture:

- **$\Omega$ (big-Omega), a lower bound.** $T(n)$ is $\Omega(f(n))$ if there exist positive constants $c$ and $n_0$ such that $T(n) \ge c \cdot f(n)$ for all $n \ge n_0$. It says that $T(n)$ grows **at least** as fast as $f(n)$.
- **$\Theta$ (big-Theta), an exact order.** $T(n)$ is $\Theta(f(n))$ if it is both $O(f(n))$ and $\Omega(f(n))$, that is, if there exist positive constants $c_1$, $c_2$ and $n_0$ such that $c_1 \cdot f(n) \le T(n) \le c_2 \cdot f(n)$ for all $n \ge n_0$. It says that $T(n)$ grows **exactly** at the rate of $f(n)$, up to constants.

| Notation | Meaning | Analogy | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $T(n) \in O(f(n))$ | grows at most as fast as $f(n)$ | $\le$ | $3n + 4 \in O(n^2)$ (true, but not tight) |
| $T(n) \in \Omega(f(n))$ | grows at least as fast as $f(n)$ | $\ge$ | $3n + 4 \in \Omega(n)$ |
| $T(n) \in \Theta(f(n))$ | grows exactly as fast as $f(n)$ | $=$ | $3n + 4 \in \Theta(n)$ |

These notations also allow us to talk about **problems**, not only algorithms. For example, we saw in the case study that any algorithm for the maximum pairwise product must read all the elements, so every algorithm for that problem takes $\Omega(n)$ time in the worst case. Since the linear scan is $O(n)$, it is **optimal**: no algorithm can have a better growth rate. Lower bounds are what allow us to measure the **inherent difficulty of a problem**.

:::info Notation in practice
In everyday use (and in this course), people often write $O$ when they actually mean $\Theta$: "the sum of a vector is $O(n)$" is usually intended as "it grows exactly linearly". Also, although $O(f(n))$ is formally a **set** of functions, you will commonly see $T(n) = O(f(n))$ instead of $T(n) \in O(f(n))$. Read the "$=$" as "is", not as a true equality: $n = O(n^2)$ is correct, but $O(n^2) = n$ makes no sense.
:::

## Why Constants Can Be Ignored (and When They Matter)

With asymptotic notation, we can evaluate a program by its growth rate, ignoring constants: a program that is $O(n)$ will be better than one that is $O(n^2)$ **for large enough inputs**.

However, there are values of $n$ for which an algorithm with time $100n$ behaves **worse** than one with time $n^2$: as long as $n \lt 100$, $n^2$ is smaller than $100n$. Asymptotic notation guarantees that the linear algorithm wins eventually, not that it wins for every input size.

In practice:

- For **large inputs**, the growth rate is decisive, and the constants become irrelevant.
- For **small inputs**, constants can matter, and a "worse" algorithm may be faster. This is why, for example, efficient sorting libraries often switch to a simple quadratic algorithm (insertion sort) for very small subarrays.

To facilitate the analysis, the values of the constants are usually removed. But a good engineer remembers that they exist.

## Properties of the Asymptotic Notation

The following properties allow us to simplify expressions without returning to the definition every time:

1. **Reflexivity.** $T(n)$ is $O(T(n))$. Every function is a bound of itself (take $c = 1$).
2. **Constant factors.** $O(c \cdot f(n)) = O(f(n))$ for any constant $c \gt 0$. Multiplying by a constant does not change the class.
3. **Sum rule.** If $T(n) = T_1(n) + T_2(n) + \cdots + T_m(n)$, with $m$ fixed, then $T(n)$ is of the order of the **largest** term: $O(\max(T_1(n), T_2(n), \dots, T_m(n)))$. This is the rule for **sequential** code: the slowest part dominates.
4. **Product rule.** If $T(n) = T_1(n) \cdot T_2(n) \cdots T_m(n)$, then $T(n)$ is $O(T_1(n) \cdot T_2(n) \cdots T_m(n))$. This is the rule for **nested** code: a loop of $O(n)$ iterations whose body costs $O(n)$ is $O(n^2)$.
5. **Base of logarithms.** $O(\log_a n) = O(\log_b n) = O(\log n)$ for any bases $a, b \gt 1$.
6. **Powers inside a logarithm.** $O(\log(n^k)) = O(k \log n) = O(\log n)$ for any constant $k$.
7. **Powers of a logarithm.** $O(\log^k n) \ne O(\log n)$ for $k \gt 1$. Here $\log^k n$ means $(\log n)^k$, which grows faster than $\log n$.

Properties 5, 6 and 7 deserve a closer look, because logarithms appear constantly in algorithm analysis.

### Why the base of the logarithm does not matter

The change-of-base formula says that, for any bases $a, b \gt 1$:

$$
\log_a n = \frac{\log_b n}{\log_b a} = \frac{1}{\log_b a} \cdot \log_b n
$$

Since $\frac{1}{\log_b a}$ is a **constant**, logarithms in different bases differ only by a constant factor, and property 2 tells us that constant factors do not change the class. That is why we simply write $O(\log n)$. In computing, when a base is needed, it is usually 2, because many algorithms halve the problem at each step.

### Power inside versus power outside

Compare $\log(n^8)$ and $(\log n)^8$:

- $\log(n^8) = 8 \log n$, which is just a constant times $\log n$, so it is $O(\log n)$ (property 6).
- $(\log n)^8$ is the logarithm **raised** to the eighth power. It grows faster than any constant times $\log n$, so it is **not** $O(\log n)$ (property 7). It is still much smaller than any power of $n$: even $(\log n)^8$ is eventually smaller than $\sqrt{n}$, although only for very large $n$.

## Logarithm Rules to Remember

These identities, valid for positive values and bases different from 1, are used continuously when analyzing algorithms:

1. $\log_a(n^k) = k \cdot \log_a n$
2. $\log_a(n \cdot m) = \log_a n + \log_a m$
3. $\log_a(n / m) = \log_a n - \log_a m$
4. $n^{\log_a b} = b^{\log_a n}$
5. $\log_a b \cdot \log_b n = \log_a n$

Rule 4 is less familiar but very useful, especially in divide and conquer: it allows us to convert an expression like $3^{\log_2 n}$ into $n^{\log_2 3} \approx n^{1.585}$. You can prove it by taking $\log_a$ of both sides: both become $\log_a n \cdot \log_a b$.

### Check your understanding

<details>
  <summary>🧠 <strong>Is $n^{\log_3 a} = a^{\log_3 n}$?</strong></summary>

  **Yes.** It is rule 4 with base 3. Taking $\log_3$ of both sides gives $\log_3 a \cdot \log_3 n$ in both cases.

</details>

<details>
  <summary>🧠 <strong>Is $O(\log_3 (n^8)) = O(8 \log_3 n)$? And $O((\log_3 n)^8) = O(8 \log_3 n)$?</strong></summary>

  The first one, **yes**: by rule 1, $\log_3(n^8) = 8 \log_3 n$, and both are $O(\log n)$. The second one, **no**: $(\log_3 n)^8$ is a power of the logarithm and grows faster than $\log n$ (property 7). Always pay attention to whether the exponent applies to $n$ or to the logarithm.

</details>

<details>
  <summary>🧠 <strong>Is $\log_3(4n) = \log_3 4 \cdot \log_3 n$?</strong></summary>

  **No.** The logarithm of a product is the **sum** of the logarithms (rule 2): $\log_3(4n) = \log_3 4 + \log_3 n$. Asymptotically, it is still $O(\log n)$, because $\log_3 4$ is just a constant added to $\log_3 n$.

</details>

<details>
  <summary>🧠 <strong>Is $\log_3 4 \cdot \log_4 n = \log_3 n$?</strong></summary>

  **Yes.** It is rule 5 with $a = 3$ and $b = 4$, a form of the change-of-base formula.

</details>

## Determining the Order of an Expression

In practice, finding the order of an expression consists of identifying its **dominant term** and dropping its constant factor. To identify the dominant term, it helps to remember the order of growth of the most common functions, from slowest to fastest:

$$
1 \;\lt\; \log n \;\lt\; \log^2 n \;\lt\; \sqrt{n} \;\lt\; n \;\lt\; n \log n \;\lt\; n^2 \;\lt\; n^3 \;\lt\; 2^n \;\lt\; n! \;\lt\; n^n
$$

and two general facts:

- **Any power of $n$** (even a tiny one, such as $n^{0.1}$) eventually grows faster than **any power of $\log n$**.
- **Any exponential** $c^n$ with $c \gt 1$ eventually grows faster than **any polynomial** $n^k$.

Some of these examples contain a trap:

- $100n + \frac{3}{10000} n \log n$: the coefficient of $n \log n$ is tiny and the coefficient of $n$ is large, but $n \log n$ still grows faster. Constants never change the class; they only change **when** the dominant term takes over (here, for astronomically large $n$).
- $n^2 + 4(n + \log n)^2 \cdot n$: expanding $(n + \log n)^2 = n^2 + 2n \log n + \log^2 n$ and multiplying by $n$ gives a dominant term $4n^3$.
- $\frac{4n^2 + 2 \log n}{n + 1}$: for large $n$, this behaves like $\frac{4n^2}{n} = 4n$.
- $16 \log n + n^{0.4}$: even a small power of $n$ beats a logarithm.
- $n \log n + 10^4 n + 10^{100}$: the constant $10^{100}$ is enormous, but it is still a constant.
- $5n^{1.6} + 10 n \log n$: compare $n^{1.6}$ with $n \log n$ by dividing both by $n$: $n^{0.6}$ grows faster than $\log n$.

</TabItem>

## Summary

- Asymptotic notation describes the **growth rate** of a function, ignoring constant factors and the behavior for small $n$.
- $T(n) \in O(f(n))$ means $T(n) \le c \cdot f(n)$ from some $n_0$ onwards: an **upper bound**. $\Omega$ is a **lower bound** and $\Theta$ an **exact order**.
- Asymptotic bounds and the best, worst and average cases are **independent** ideas.
- Lower bounds on problems let us prove that an algorithm is **optimal**.
- Key properties: constants are dropped, a sum is dominated by its largest term, products multiply, and the base of logarithms is irrelevant, but powers of logarithms are not.
- To find the order of an expression, identify the dominant term using the hierarchy of functions.
