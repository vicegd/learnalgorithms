Brute Force and Exhaustive Search

Brute force is the most direct way to design an algorithm: solve the problem by doing exactly what its statement says, with no attempt to exploit its structure. To find an element, look at every position. To find the best combination, generate every combination and keep the best one. To check whether a text contains a word, try the word at every position of the text.

Choosing a Design Technique

You have now seen six design techniques you have a problem, and you must decide which technique to use.

The Backtracking Scheme

Some problems resist every technique seen so far. There is no greedy criterion that guarantees the optimum, the problem does not split into independent halves, and there is no small table of subproblems to fill. Very often the only way to be sure of the answer is to examine the candidate solutions one by one. Backtracking is the paradigm that organizes that examination so that it is systematic (no candidate is forgotten) and as cheap as possible (whole families of candidates are discarded at once).

The Branch and Bound Scheme

Branch and bound (B&B) is the last design technique of the course. Like backtracking, it searches an implicit tree of states: nodes represent states of the problem, edges represent valid changes, and solving the problem means finding a node (or a path) in that tree. The difference is the order in which the tree is explored, and that single change makes it suitable for different problems.

The Divide and Conquer Scheme

Divide and conquer is one of the oldest and most powerful strategies for designing algorithms. Instead of attacking a problem as a whole, it breaks it into smaller problems of the same kind, solves them (usually by applying the same strategy again), and combines their solutions. We have already met two examples: binary search, which discards half of the array at each step, and Quicksort, which partitions the array and sorts each part.

The Dynamic Programming Method

Divide and conquer breaks a problem into subproblems and combines their solutions. It works beautifully when the subproblems are independent. But we saw in the divide and conquer chapter that the naive recursive Fibonacci solves the same subproblems again and again, and becomes exponential. Dynamic programming keeps the idea of solving a problem through its subproblems, but solves each subproblem only once, storing its solution in a table for later use.

The Greedy Method

Many problems ask not just for a solution, but for the best one they build the solution step by step and, at each step, they take the choice that looks best right now, without ever reconsidering it.
