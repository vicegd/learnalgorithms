Analyzing Divide and Conquer

The loops of iterative algorithms can be analyzed by counting iterations, as we did in the first chapter. Recursive algorithms need a different tool: the execution time is expressed in terms of itself applied to smaller sizes, giving a recurrence equation. Fortunately, most divide and conquer algorithms follow one of two very regular patterns, and for both of them the solution is known in general. This lesson presents those two patterns, explains where their formulas come from, and shows how to apply them.

Induction, Correctness and Recurrences

Recursive algorithms raise two questions: is the algorithm correct, and how long does it take? The answer to both uses the same idea. A recursive algorithm solves a problem by trusting that the smaller calls work; mathematical induction is the tool that makes that trust rigorous, and a recurrence is the equation that describes its cost in terms of the cost of the smaller calls.

Proofs: Dynamic Programming

A dynamic programming algorithm is correct if its recurrence is correct: the optimal value of each subproblem must be obtained from the optimal values of smaller subproblems. This page proves the recurrences used in the lessons.
