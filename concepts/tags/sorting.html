Beyond Quadratic Sorting

Quicksort is not the only way to sort faster than $O(n^2)$. Each of the three basic ideas (exchange, insertion and selection) has an efficient counterpart, and there are also algorithms based on completely different principles. This lesson presents the most important ones, explains the theoretical limit that all comparison-based algorithms share, and shows how some algorithms manage to go below that limit.

Bubble Sort

Bubble sort is probably the best-known sorting algorithm, and also one of the least efficient. It is worth studying precisely because it is so simple: it lets us practise the exact analysis of comparisons and moves, introduces the important idea of inversions, and shows how small changes (a stop condition, a change of direction) affect the behavior without changing the complexity.

Insertion Sort

Insertion sort is the method most people use, without thinking, to sort a hand of cards it performs fewer comparisons and moves, and it is extremely fast on data that is already almost sorted. For this reason, it is used inside many professional sorting algorithms.

This page proves the claims made in the sorting lessons: that each algorithm sorts correctly, the costs given in the lessons, and the limits that no comparison-based algorithm can break. The proofs use loop invariants and induction, explained in Induction, Correctness and Recurrences. Arrays are indexed from 0 and have $n$ elements.

Quicksort

Quicksort, invented by Tony Hoare around 1960, is one of the most important algorithms ever designed. It is the basis of the sorting routines of many programming languages and libraries, and it is usually the fastest general-purpose sorting algorithm in practice. It improves the idea of exchange: instead of exchanging adjacent elements, it exchanges elements that are far apart, removing many inversions at once.

Selection Sort

Selection sort follows the most direct strategy imaginable: find the smallest element and put it first; then find the smallest of the rest and put it second; and so on. It is the simple algorithm with the most predictable behavior, the one that performs the fewest exchanges, and, unlike the previous two, it is not stable.

The Sorting Problem

Sorting is one of the most frequent operations in any information system, and one of the most thoroughly studied problems in computer science. When there is a certain volume of data, keeping it sorted makes searching (and many other operations) much faster: we saw in the previous chapter that binary search needs about 20 steps to search a million sorted elements, while sequential search may need a million.
