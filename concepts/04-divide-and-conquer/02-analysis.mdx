# Analyzing Divide and Conquer Algorithms

The loops of iterative algorithms can be analyzed by counting iterations, as we did in the first chapter. Recursive algorithms need a different tool: the execution time is expressed in terms of **itself** applied to smaller sizes, giving a **recurrence equation**. Fortunately, most divide and conquer algorithms follow one of two very regular patterns, and for both of them the solution is known in general. This lesson presents those two patterns, explains where their formulas come from, and shows how to apply them.

:::note What you will learn in this lesson
- How to model a divide and conquer algorithm with the parameters $a$, $b$ and $k$.
- The complexity formulas for **division** and **subtraction** schemes, and the intuition behind them (the recursion tree).
- How to identify $a$, $b$ and $k$ in real code, and the most common mistakes.
- The limits of these formulas.
:::

---

## The Parameters $a$, $b$ and $k$

Both schemes are described by three parameters:

- $a$: the **number of subproblems** (recursive calls) executed in each invocation;
- $b$: how the size decreases: each subproblem has size $n / b$ (**division**) or $n - b$ (**subtraction**), where $b$ is a constant;
- $k$: the cost of the work done in each invocation **excluding the recursive calls** (dividing and combining) is $O(n^k)$, a polynomial.

The base case takes constant time (or, more generally, $c \cdot n^k$ for its small size).

---

## Division Scheme

The execution time satisfies:

$$
T(n) = \begin{cases} a \cdot T(n/b) + c \cdot n^k & \text{if } n \text{ is greater than the base case} \\ c \cdot n^k & \text{in the base case} \end{cases}
$$

and its solution is:

$$
T(n) \in \begin{cases} O(n^k) & \text{if } a \lt b^k \\ O(n^k \log n) & \text{if } a = b^k \\ O(n^{\log_b a}) & \text{if } a \gt b^k \end{cases}
$$

This result is a particular case of the so-called **master theorem**.

### Where does it come from? The recursion tree

Draw the tree of recursive calls. The root is the original problem, of size $n$. It has $a$ children of size $n/b$, each of which has $a$ children of size $n/b^2$, and so on, until the base case. The tree has about $\log_b n$ levels.

<Figure caption="The recursion tree of the division scheme. Level i has aⁱ subproblems of size n/bⁱ; multiplying the number of subproblems by the work of each one gives the work of the level.">
  <FigRecursionTree />
</Figure>

| Level | Number of subproblems | Size of each one | Non-recursive work at the level |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| 0 | $1$ | $n$ | $c \cdot n^k$ |
| 1 | $a$ | $n/b$ | $a \cdot c \cdot (n/b)^k = c \cdot n^k \cdot \frac{a}{b^k}$ |
| 2 | $a^2$ | $n/b^2$ | $c \cdot n^k \cdot \left(\frac{a}{b^k}\right)^2$ |
| $i$ | $a^i$ | $n/b^i$ | $c \cdot n^k \cdot \left(\frac{a}{b^k}\right)^i$ |
| $\log_b n$ (leaves) | $a^{\log_b n} = n^{\log_b a}$ | constant | proportional to $n^{\log_b a}$ |

The total time is the sum of all levels: $c \cdot n^k$ multiplied by a **geometric series** of ratio $r = \frac{a}{b^k}$. Everything depends on that ratio:

- **$a \lt b^k$ ($r \lt 1$):** the work **decreases** from each level to the next. The sum is dominated by the first term: the work at the root, $O(n^k)$. The subproblems shrink faster than they multiply.
- **$a = b^k$ ($r = 1$):** every level does the **same** work, $c \cdot n^k$, and there are $\log_b n$ levels: $O(n^k \log n)$.
- **$a \gt b^k$ ($r \gt 1$):** the work **increases** from each level to the next. The sum is dominated by the last level, the leaves, whose number is $a^{\log_b n}$. By the logarithm rule $x^{\log_b y} = y^{\log_b x}$ from the asymptotic notation lesson, $a^{\log_b n} = n^{\log_b a}$: $O(n^{\log_b a})$.

<Figure caption="The work of each level of the tree in the three cases. The ratio a/bᵏ decides whether the root, all the levels equally, or the leaves dominate the total.">
  <FigCostProfiles />
</Figure>

So the three cases correspond to three situations: the cost is concentrated at the **top** of the tree, **evenly spread** over all levels, or concentrated at the **bottom**.

## Subtraction Scheme

The execution time satisfies:

$$
T(n) = \begin{cases} a \cdot T(n - b) + c \cdot n^k & \text{if } n \text{ is greater than the base case} \\ c \cdot n^k & \text{in the base case} \end{cases}
$$

and its solution is:

$$
T(n) \in \begin{cases} O(n^{k+1}) & \text{if } a = 1 \\ O(a^{n / b}) & \text{if } a \gt 1 \end{cases}
$$

(Some textbooks also list a case $a \lt 1$, but it cannot happen in practice: a recursive algorithm makes at least one call in its general case.)

The intuition is again in the recursion tree, which now has about $n / b$ levels:

- **$a = 1$:** a single chain of $n/b$ calls, with sizes $n, n - b, n - 2b, \dots$. The work at each one is at most $c \cdot n^k$, and there are $n/b$ of them: $O(n^{k+1})$. A loop of $n$ iterations whose body costs $O(n^k)$, written recursively.
- **$a \gt 1$:** the number of calls **multiplies by $a$ at each level**, through $n/b$ levels: $a^{n/b}$ calls. The algorithm is **exponential**, and the non-recursive work does not even matter.

<Figure caption="The recursion trees of the subtraction scheme. With one recursive call the calls form a chain of n/b links; with two, the number of calls doubles at every level.">
  <FigSubtractionTrees />
</Figure>

:::danger Avoid subtraction with several subproblems
An algorithm with $a \gt 1$ in a subtraction scheme is exponential, and therefore unusable except for very small inputs. If you find yourself writing two or more recursive calls on $n - 1$ (or $n - 2$...), stop and think: most likely the same subproblems are being solved many times, and a different technique (an iterative version, or dynamic programming) is needed.
:::

## Identifying $a$, $b$ and $k$

To apply the formulas, look at the code of the recursive method and answer three questions:

1. **How many recursive calls are executed** in one invocation (in the general case)? That is $a$. Count the calls that are actually executed, not the ones that are written: in binary search there are two calls in the code, but only one of them runs in each invocation, so $a = 1$. And a call inside a loop that runs $m$ times counts $m$ times.
2. **What is the size of each recursive call?** If it is $n/b$, the scheme is division; if it is $n - b$, subtraction.
3. **What is the cost of everything else** (loops, copies, partitions, merges)? If it is $O(n^k)$, that gives $k$. A constant amount of work means $k = 0$.

For example, in Mergesort:

```java
public void mergesort(int[] v, int left, int right) {
    if (left < right) {                       // base case: one element, constant time
        int center = (left + right) / 2;      // O(1)
        mergesort(v, left, center);           // call 1, on half the elements
        mergesort(v, center + 1, right);      // call 2, on the other half
        merge(v, left, center, right);        // O(n): goes through the n elements once
    }
}
```

Two calls are executed ($a = 2$), each one on half of the elements ($b = 2$, division), and the rest of the work is dominated by the linear merge ($k = 1$). Since $a = 2 = b^k = 2^1$, Mergesort is $O(n \log n)$.

The naive Fibonacci does not fit the scheme exactly, because its two calls have different sizes, $n - 1$ and $n - 2$. Replacing $n - 2$ by $n - 1$ can only make the tree larger, so the scheme with $a = 2$, $b = 1$ gives an **upper bound**, $O(2^n)$. The exact growth is about $1.618^n$, still exponential.

Notice how Quicksort appears in both tables: with perfectly balanced partitions, it follows a division scheme ($a = 2$, $b = 2$, $k = 1$); with the most unbalanced ones, a subtraction scheme in which one of the two parts is empty, so only one non-trivial call remains ($a = 1$, $b = 1$, $k = 1$). Both formulas confirm what we found in the sorting chapter: $O(n \log n)$ in the best case and $O(n^2)$ in the worst.

  **Question:** Give the complexity of (1) an algorithm that splits a problem of size $n$ into 8 subproblems of size $n/2$ and combines them in $O(n^2)$, and (2) the same with 7 subproblems.

  **Answer:** (1) $a = 8$, $b = 2$, $k = 2$: $a = 8 \gt b^k = 4$, so $O(n^{\log_2 8}) = O(n^3)$. (2) With $a = 7$: $O(n^{\log_2 7}) \approx O(n^{2.81})$. This is **Strassen's algorithm**, which saves one block multiplication at the cost of more additions, and beats the classic cubic algorithm for large matrices. The same trick, saving one recursive call, makes Karatsuba's multiplication faster.

## How Each Parameter Affects the Cost

The formulas give useful design guidance:

- **Reducing $a$** (fewer subproblems) is often the key to a faster algorithm when $a \gt b^k$: Karatsuba (4 to 3 products) and Strassen (8 to 7) are famous examples.
- **Reducing $k$** (cheaper division and combination) helps when $a \le b^k$. For example, Mergesort with an $O(n^2)$ merge would be $O(n^2)$ instead of $O(n \log n)$.
- **Division beats subtraction**: dividing the size by 2 instead of subtracting 1 turns $O(n)$ levels into $O(\log n)$, and can turn an exponential algorithm into a polynomial one.

---

## Limits of the Formulas

These formulas cover the most common cases, but not all of them:

- **Subproblems of different sizes.** If an algorithm divides into parts of sizes $n/3$ and $2n/3$, or into parts whose size depends on the data (Quicksort with random pivots, Quickselect), the formulas do not apply directly. The recursion tree, or probabilistic analysis, is needed. For example, Quicksort's average case is $O(n \log n)$, and Quickselect's average case is $O(n)$, although neither follows exactly any of the two schemes.
- **Non-polynomial non-recursive work.** If dividing and combining costs, for example, $O(n \log n)$, $k$ is not an integer power. The recurrence $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n \log n$ has solution $O(n \log^2 n)$, which the simple formulas do not give. The general master theorem covers many of these cases.
- **Exact constants.** The formulas give the order of growth, not the exact time.

When an algorithm does not fit the schemes, the safest method is to draw the recursion tree, compute the work at each level and add it up, as we did above.

## Predicting Execution Times

Once the complexity is known, the techniques of the first chapter apply directly. For example, if Mergesort ($O(n \log n)$) takes 2 seconds for $n = 2^{20}$, for $n = 2^{22}$ it will take about

$$
t_2 = \frac{2^{22} \cdot 22}{2^{20} \cdot 20} \cdot 2 \approx 8.8 \text{ seconds}
$$

And if the Towers of Hanoi ($O(2^n)$) take 1 second for 20 discs, then 30 discs will take about $2^{10} = 1{,}024$ seconds (17 minutes), and 64 discs, about $2^{44}$ seconds: more than 500,000 years.

## Summary

- Divide and conquer algorithms are analyzed with **recurrences**, characterised by $a$ (number of subproblems), $b$ (how the size decreases) and $k$ (cost of dividing and combining, $O(n^k)$).
- **Division** ($n/b$): $O(n^k)$ if $a \lt b^k$; $O(n^k \log n)$ if $a = b^k$; $O(n^{\log_b a})$ if $a \gt b^k$.
- **Subtraction** ($n - b$): $O(n^{k+1})$ if $a = 1$; $O(a^{n/b})$, exponential, if $a \gt 1$.
- The **recursion tree** explains the formulas: the cost is concentrated at the top, evenly spread, or concentrated at the leaves.
- Count the recursive calls that are **executed**, not the ones written. Subproblems of unequal or data-dependent size need a specific analysis.
