# Summary: Divide and Conquer

Everything on one page, to review the chapter before moving on.

## Key ideas

- Divide and conquer **divides** a problem into smaller subproblems of the same kind, **solves** them recursively and **combines** their solutions ([The Divide and Conquer Scheme](./01-divide-and-conquer-scheme.mdx)).
- The number of subproblems should be small, and the subproblems **independent**. When they repeat, dynamic programming is the right tool.
- With a single subproblem ($a = 1$) the process is a **reduction**, often better written as a loop. Recursion uses stack memory proportional to its depth.
- Count the recursive calls that are **executed**, not the ones written ([Analyzing Divide and Conquer](./02-analysis.mdx)).
- Discarding the subproblems that cannot contain the answer is what makes binary search and Quickselect fast ([Majority, Mode and Median](./05-majority-mode-and-median.mdx)).

## Solving the recurrences

With $a$ subproblems and a cost of dividing and combining in $O(n^k)$ ([Analyzing Divide and Conquer](./02-analysis.mdx)):

| Scheme | Condition | Complexity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Division ($n/b$) | $a \lt b^k$ | $O(n^k)$ |
| Division ($n/b$) | $a = b^k$ | $O(n^k \log n)$ |
| Division ($n/b$) | $a \gt b^k$ | $O(n^{\log_b a})$ |
| Subtraction ($n - b$) | $a = 1$ | $O(n^{k+1})$ |
| Subtraction ($n - b$) | $a \gt 1$ | $O(a^{n/b})$, exponential |

## When to use it

- The problem splits into **independent** subproblems of the same kind, and their solutions combine cheaply.
- Avoid it when the subproblems **overlap** (naive Fibonacci): use dynamic programming instead.
- Prefer division schemes ($n/b$) to subtraction schemes ($n - b$) with several subproblems, which are exponential.
