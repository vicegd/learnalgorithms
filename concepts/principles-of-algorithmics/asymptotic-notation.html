Asymptotic Notation: Describing How Cost Grows

⏱ 14 min readRead first: Complexity Analysis

In the previous lesson we obtained exact expressions such as T(n)=3n+4T(n) = 3n + 4T(n)=3n+4 and saw that the constants depend on arbitrary counting decisions and, in reality, on the machine. What does not depend on them is the rate of growth of the function: how fast T(n)T(n)T(n) increases when nnn becomes large.

Asymptotic notation is the mathematical language for describing that rate of growth. It refers to the speed of growth of the values of a function f(n)f(n)f(n) as nnn tends to infinity, ignoring constant factors and lower-order terms. It is the notation used in every textbook, paper and technical interview to talk about the efficiency of algorithms.

What you will learn in this lesson
  • The formal definition of OOO (big-O) and how to prove that a function belongs to O(f(n))O(f(n))O(f(n)).
  • The related notations Ω\OmegaΩ (big-Omega) and Θ\ThetaΘ (big-Theta), and what each one really says.
  • The algebraic properties that let us simplify expressions without going back to the definition.
  • The logarithm rules you need for analyzing algorithms, and why the base of a logarithm does not matter.
  • How to determine the order of complex expressions quickly and reliably.

Big-O: An Upper Bound on Growth​

Definition. A function T(n)T(n)T(n) is O(f(n))O(f(n))O(f(n)) if there exist two positive constants ccc and n0n_0n0​ such that:

T(n)≤c⋅f(n)for all n≥n0T(n) \le c \cdot f(n) \qquad \text{for all } n \ge n_0T(n)≤c⋅f(n)for all n≥n0​

In words: from some point n0n_0n0​ onwards, T(n)T(n)T(n) never exceeds a constant multiple of f(n)f(n)f(n). If T(n)T(n)T(n) is O(f(n))O(f(n))O(f(n)), we say that T(n)T(n)T(n) has, at most, a growth rate of f(n)f(n)f(n).

Two details of the definition are important:

  • The constant ccc absorbs the constant factors: 3n3n3n, 100n100n100n and n/2n/2n/2 are all O(n)O(n)O(n). This is exactly the freedom we need, since constants depend on the machine and on the counting conventions.
  • The threshold n0n_0n0​ means that only the behavior for large values of nnn matters. What happens for small inputs is irrelevant to the definition.

Proving a bound: an example​

Let us prove that T(n)=5n4+4n3T(n) = 5n^4 + 4n^3T(n)=5n4+4n3 is O(n4)O(n^4)O(n4). We need constants ccc and n0n_0n0​ such that 5n4+4n3≤c⋅n45n^4 + 4n^3 \le c \cdot n^45n4+4n3≤c⋅n4 for every n≥n0n \ge n_0n≥n0​.

The idea is to bound the lower-order term by the dominant one. For n≥4n \ge 4n≥4 we have 4n3≤n⋅n3=n44n^3 \le n \cdot n^3 = n^44n3≤n⋅n3=n4, therefore:

5n4+4n3≤5n4+n4=6n4for all n≥45n^4 + 4n^3 \le 5n^4 + n^4 = 6n^4 \qquad \text{for all } n \ge 45n4+4n3≤5n4+n4=6n4for all n≥4

So the definition holds with c=6c = 6c=6 and n0=4n_0 = 4n0​=4 (or any larger n0n_0n0​, such as n0=5n_0 = 5n0​=5). The values of ccc and n0n_0n0​ are not unique: with c=9c = 9c=9, for example, the inequality holds from n0=1n_0 = 1n0​=1, because 4n3≤4n44n^3 \le 4n^44n3≤4n4 for n≥1n \ge 1n≥1. We only need to show that some pair exists.

A quicker test with limits

When the limit exists, we can compare growth rates with:

lim⁡n→∞T(n)f(n)=L\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{T(n)}{f(n)} = Ln→∞lim​f(n)T(n)​=L
  • If LLL is a finite number (including 0), then T(n)T(n)T(n) is O(f(n))O(f(n))O(f(n)).
  • If L=0L = 0L=0, T(n)T(n)T(n) grows strictly more slowly than f(n)f(n)f(n).
  • If LLL is a finite positive number, both functions grow at the same rate.
  • If L=∞L = \inftyL=∞, T(n)T(n)T(n) grows faster, and it is not O(f(n))O(f(n))O(f(n)).

For example, lim⁡n→∞5n4+4n3n4=lim⁡n→∞(5+4n)=5\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{5n^4 + 4n^3}{n^4} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(5 + \frac{4}{n}\right) = 5limn→∞​n45n4+4n3​=limn→∞​(5+n4​)=5, so both functions grow at the same rate.

Big-O is a bound, not an exact description​

Since OOO is an upper bound, a function belongs to many classes: 3n+43n + 43n+4 is O(n)O(n)O(n), but also O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) and O(2n)O(2^n)O(2n), because all those functions grow at least as fast as nnn. These statements are true but not very informative. When we analyze an algorithm, we always give the tightest bound we can prove: saying that the sum of a vector is O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) is correct, but misleading.

Big-O does not mean "worst case"

A common misconception is that OOO describes the worst case. It does not: OOO, Ω\OmegaΩ and Θ\ThetaΘ describe functions, and any of the functions we studied in the previous lesson can be described with them. The worst-case time of linear search is O(n)O(n)O(n), its best-case time is O(1)O(1)O(1), its average-case time is O(n)O(n)O(n). When someone says that "an algorithm is O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2)" without further detail, they usually mean that its worst-case time is O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2), but the two ideas are independent.

Big-Omega and Big-Theta​

Big-O gives an upper bound. Two related notations complete the picture:

  • Ω\OmegaΩ (big-Omega), a lower bound. T(n)T(n)T(n) is Ω(f(n))\Omega(f(n))Ω(f(n)) if there exist positive constants ccc and n0n_0n0​ such that T(n)≥c⋅f(n)T(n) \ge c \cdot f(n)T(n)≥c⋅f(n) for all n≥n0n \ge n_0n≥n0​. It says that T(n)T(n)T(n) grows at least as fast as f(n)f(n)f(n).
  • Θ\ThetaΘ (big-Theta), an exact order. T(n)T(n)T(n) is Θ(f(n))\Theta(f(n))Θ(f(n)) if it is both O(f(n))O(f(n))O(f(n)) and Ω(f(n))\Omega(f(n))Ω(f(n)), that is, if there exist positive constants c1c_1c1​, c2c_2c2​ and n0n_0n0​ such that c1⋅f(n)≤T(n)≤c2⋅f(n)c_1 \cdot f(n) \le T(n) \le c_2 \cdot f(n)c1​⋅f(n)≤T(n)≤c2​⋅f(n) for all n≥n0n \ge n_0n≥n0​. It says that T(n)T(n)T(n) grows exactly at the rate of f(n)f(n)f(n), up to constants.

These notations also allow us to talk about problems, not only algorithms. For example, we saw in the case study that any algorithm for the maximum pairwise product must read all the elements, so every algorithm for that problem takes Ω(n)\Omega(n)Ω(n) time in the worst case. Since the linear scan is O(n)O(n)O(n), it is optimal: no algorithm can have a better growth rate. Lower bounds are what allow us to measure the inherent difficulty of a problem.

Notation in practice

In everyday use (and in this course), people often write OOO when they actually mean Θ\ThetaΘ: "the sum of a vector is O(n)O(n)O(n)" is usually intended as "it grows exactly linearly". Also, although O(f(n))O(f(n))O(f(n)) is formally a set of functions, you will commonly see T(n)=O(f(n))T(n) = O(f(n))T(n)=O(f(n)) instead of T(n)∈O(f(n))T(n) \in O(f(n))T(n)∈O(f(n)). Read the "===" as "is", not as a true equality: n=O(n2)n = O(n^2)n=O(n2) is correct, but O(n2)=nO(n^2) = nO(n2)=n makes no sense.

Why Constants Can Be Ignored (and When They Matter)​

With asymptotic notation, we can evaluate a program by its growth rate, ignoring constants: a program that is O(n)O(n)O(n) will be better than one that is O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) for large enough inputs.

However, there are values of nnn for which an algorithm with time 100n100n100n behaves worse than one with time n2n^2n2: as long as n<100n \lt 100n<100, n2n^2n2 is smaller than 100n100n100n. Asymptotic notation guarantees that the linear algorithm wins eventually, not that it wins for every input size.

In practice:

  • For large inputs, the growth rate is decisive, and the constants become irrelevant.
  • For small inputs, constants can matter, and a "worse" algorithm may be faster. This is why, for example, efficient sorting libraries often switch to a simple quadratic algorithm (insertion sort) for very small subarrays.

To facilitate the analysis, the values of the constants are usually removed. But a good engineer remembers that they exist.

Properties of the Asymptotic Notation​

The following properties allow us to simplify expressions without returning to the definition every time:

  1. Reflexivity. T(n)T(n)T(n) is O(T(n))O(T(n))O(T(n)). Every function is a bound of itself (take c=1c = 1c=1).
  2. Constant factors. O(c⋅f(n))=O(f(n))O(c \cdot f(n)) = O(f(n))O(c⋅f(n))=O(f(n)) for any constant c>0c \gt 0c>0. Multiplying by a constant does not change the class.
  3. Sum rule. If T(n)=T1(n)+T2(n)+⋯+Tm(n)T(n) = T_1(n) + T_2(n) + \cdots + T_m(n)T(n)=T1​(n)+T2​(n)+⋯+Tm​(n), with mmm fixed, then T(n)T(n)T(n) is of the order of the largest term: O(max⁡(T1(n),T2(n),…,Tm(n)))O(\max(T_1(n), T_2(n), \dots, T_m(n)))O(max(T1​(n),T2​(n),…,Tm​(n))). This is the rule for sequential code: the slowest part dominates.
  4. Product rule. If T(n)=T1(n)⋅T2(n)⋯Tm(n)T(n) = T_1(n) \cdot T_2(n) \cdots T_m(n)T(n)=T1​(n)⋅T2​(n)⋯Tm​(n), then T(n)T(n)T(n) is O(T1(n)⋅T2(n)⋯Tm(n))O(T_1(n) \cdot T_2(n) \cdots T_m(n))O(T1​(n)⋅T2​(n)⋯Tm​(n)). This is the rule for nested code: a loop of O(n)O(n)O(n) iterations whose body costs O(n)O(n)O(n) is O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2).
  5. Base of logarithms. O(log⁡an)=O(log⁡bn)=O(log⁡n)O(\log_a n) = O(\log_b n) = O(\log n)O(loga​n)=O(logb​n)=O(logn) for any bases a,b>1a, b \gt 1a,b>1.
  6. Powers inside a logarithm. O(log⁡(nk))=O(klog⁡n)=O(log⁡n)O(\log(n^k)) = O(k \log n) = O(\log n)O(log(nk))=O(klogn)=O(logn) for any constant kkk.
  7. Powers of a logarithm. O(log⁡kn)≠O(log⁡n)O(\log^k n) \ne O(\log n)O(logkn)=O(logn) for k>1k \gt 1k>1. Here log⁡kn\log^k nlogkn means (log⁡n)k(\log n)^k(logn)k, which grows faster than log⁡n\log nlogn.

Properties 5, 6 and 7 deserve a closer look, because logarithms appear constantly in algorithm analysis.

Why the base of the logarithm does not matter​

The change-of-base formula says that, for any bases a,b>1a, b \gt 1a,b>1:

log⁡an=log⁡bnlog⁡ba=1log⁡ba⋅log⁡bn\log_a n = \frac{\log_b n}{\log_b a} = \frac{1}{\log_b a} \cdot \log_b nloga​n=logb​alogb​n​=logb​a1​⋅logb​n

Since 1log⁡ba\frac{1}{\log_b a}logb​a1​ is a constant, logarithms in different bases differ only by a constant factor, and property 2 tells us that constant factors do not change the class. That is why we simply write O(log⁡n)O(\log n)O(logn). In computing, when a base is needed, it is usually 2, because many algorithms halve the problem at each step.

Power inside versus power outside​

Compare log⁡(n8)\log(n^8)log(n8) and (log⁡n)8(\log n)^8(logn)8:

  • log⁡(n8)=8log⁡n\log(n^8) = 8 \log nlog(n8)=8logn, which is just a constant times log⁡n\log nlogn, so it is O(log⁡n)O(\log n)O(logn) (property 6).
  • (log⁡n)8(\log n)^8(logn)8 is the logarithm raised to the eighth power. It grows faster than any constant times log⁡n\log nlogn, so it is not O(log⁡n)O(\log n)O(logn) (property 7). It is still much smaller than any power of nnn: even (log⁡n)8(\log n)^8(logn)8 is eventually smaller than n\sqrt{n}n​, although only for very large nnn.

Logarithm Rules to Remember​

These identities, valid for positive values and bases different from 1, are used continuously when analyzing algorithms:

  1. log⁡a(nk)=k⋅log⁡an\log_a(n^k) = k \cdot \log_a nloga​(nk)=k⋅loga​n
  2. log⁡a(n⋅m)=log⁡an+log⁡am\log_a(n \cdot m) = \log_a n + \log_a mloga​(n⋅m)=loga​n+loga​m
  3. log⁡a(n/m)=log⁡an−log⁡am\log_a(n / m) = \log_a n - \log_a mloga​(n/m)=loga​n−loga​m
  4. nlog⁡ab=blog⁡ann^{\log_a b} = b^{\log_a n}nloga​b=bloga​n
  5. log⁡ab⋅log⁡bn=log⁡an\log_a b \cdot \log_b n = \log_a nloga​b⋅logb​n=loga​n

Rule 4 is less familiar but very useful, especially in divide and conquer: it allows us to convert an expression like 3log⁡2n3^{\log_2 n}3log2​n into nlog⁡23≈n1.585n^{\log_2 3} \approx n^{1.585}nlog2​3≈n1.585. You can prove it by taking log⁡a\log_aloga​ of both sides: both become log⁡an⋅log⁡ab\log_a n \cdot \log_a bloga​n⋅loga​b.

Determining the Order of an Expression​

In practice, finding the order of an expression consists of identifying its dominant term and dropping its constant factor. To identify the dominant term, it helps to remember the order of growth of the most common functions, from slowest to fastest:

1  <  log⁡n  <  log⁡2n  <  n  <  n  <  nlog⁡n  <  n2  <  n3  <  2n  <  n!  <  nn1 \;\lt\; \log n \;\lt\; \log^2 n \;\lt\; \sqrt{n} \;\lt\; n \;\lt\; n \log n \;\lt\; n^2 \;\lt\; n^3 \;\lt\; 2^n \;\lt\; n! \;\lt\; n^n1<logn<log2n<n​<n<nlogn<n2<n3<2n<n!<nn

and two general facts:

  • Any power of nnn (even a tiny one, such as n0.1n^{0.1}n0.1) eventually grows faster than any power of log⁡n\log nlogn.
  • Any exponential cnc^ncn with c>1c \gt 1c>1 eventually grows faster than any polynomial nkn^knk.

However, the crossover happens for an astronomically large nnn, so for any practical input size, nlog⁡nn \log nnlogn is the smaller of the two.

Common mistakes
  • Reading OOO as an exact order. 3n∈O(n2)3n \in O(n^2)3n∈O(n2) is true but not tight; use Θ\ThetaΘ to state the exact order.
  • Identifying OOO with the worst case and Ω\OmegaΩ with the best case. Bounds and cases are independent ideas: each case has its own OOO, Ω\OmegaΩ and Θ\ThetaΘ.
  • Dropping powers of logarithms. The base of a logarithm does not matter, but log⁡2n∉O(log⁡n)\log^2 n \notin O(\log n)log2n∈/O(logn).
  • Not simplifying. Write O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2), not O(3n2+n)O(3n^2 + n)O(3n2+n) or O(2n2)O(2n^2)O(2n2).

Summary​

  • Asymptotic notation describes the growth rate of a function, ignoring constant factors and the behavior for small nnn.
  • T(n)∈O(f(n))T(n) \in O(f(n))T(n)∈O(f(n)) means T(n)≤c⋅f(n)T(n) \le c \cdot f(n)T(n)≤c⋅f(n) from some n0n_0n0​ onwards: an upper bound. Ω\OmegaΩ is a lower bound and Θ\ThetaΘ an exact order.
  • Asymptotic bounds and the best, worst and average cases are independent ideas.
  • Lower bounds on problems let us prove that an algorithm is optimal.
  • Key properties: constants are dropped, a sum is dominated by its largest term, products multiply, and the base of logarithms is irrelevant, but powers of logarithms are not.
  • To find the order of an expression, identify the dominant term using the hierarchy of functions.
